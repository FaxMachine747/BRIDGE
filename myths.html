Myth-Busting

A few common misconceptions, corrected using cited research.

Myth: Avoiding eye contact means someone is being dishonest or disinterested.

Fact: For many autistic people, looking away helps free up cognitive resources to focus on listening. It's a strategy for concentrating, not a sign of avoidance.

Myth: Taking a long time to respond means someone doesn't understand or doesn't want to talk.

Fact: Many autistic people simply need more processing time between hearing something and replying. Waiting a little longer usually gets you a fuller answer.

Myth: Blunt or very direct answers are meant to be rude.

Fact: Direct, literal communication is a valid and common autistic communication style, not an attempt to offend.

Myth: Communication problems between autistic and non-autistic people are the autistic person's responsibility to fix.

Fact: Research on the "double empathy problem" (Milton, 2012; Crompton et al., 2020) shows miscommunication is a two-way mismatch, not a one-sided deficit.

Myth: Stimming (hand-flapping, rocking, fidgeting) is something to stop or discourage.

Fact: Stimming is often a self-regulation strategy that helps manage stress or sensory input. Unless it's causing harm, it doesn't need to be interrupted or corrected.

Myth: If someone doesn't respond the way you expect, repeating yourself louder or slower will help.

Fact: The issue is usually processing time, not volume or clarity. Repeating a question immediately often adds more to process rather than making it easier.
